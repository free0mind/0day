# Goals

This document records the goals and long-term vision of **Light of the Future**.

## Primary goal: guide human evolution

The primary goal of Light of the Future is to guide human evolution. Building on humanity's long-term survival and reproduction, it seeks to advance human perception, cognition, communication, and modes of existence, enabling humanity to continually expand its capabilities and possibilities.

## Ultimate goal: dimensional advancement

The project defines the ultimate goal of human evolution as “dimensional advancement,” encompassing **informational dimensional advancement** and **physical dimensional advancement**. These directions are at different stages of exploration; their meanings, enabling conditions, and potential methods require separate study.

## Informational dimensional advancement

This direction aims to continually increase the dimensionality of how information is organized, transmitted, perceived, and thought about in the human world. Here, dimensionality initially refers to the organization and presentation of information and the ways humans process it.

### From linear language to multidimensional communication

A common form of language transmission today is a linear sequence of words or tokens. The project seeks to explore two-dimensional, three-dimensional, and higher-dimensional forms of expression and communication, presenting information through spatial relationships, parallel structures, and other multidimensional arrangements.

This requires studying how information is encoded, delivered, and understood, and how participants establish a shared cognitive basis for communication.

### From sensory input to higher-dimensional thought

For vision, the project seeks to explore ways of directly organizing and receiving three-dimensional information beyond existing two-dimensional retinal input and the brain's reconstruction of three-dimensional space. It envisions using this as a basis for higher-dimensional thought. Humans already reason through space and time; the project seeks to further expand this capacity.

Mechanical waves carrying sound and electromagnetic waves such as light are also within this scope. These carriers have multiple describable properties, including time, space, frequency, and amplitude. The project explores how these properties could organize and transmit multidimensional information, and how humans could perceive and process it.

### Multidimensionality of the soul

The long-term vision also includes exploring multidimensionality of the human “soul.” The meaning of this expression, associated observable phenomena, and methods of study remain topics for future discussion. No implementation mechanism is presumed at this stage.

## Physical dimensional advancement

Physical dimensional advancement is a more distant direction. The project considers it premature to formulate concrete objectives or implementation plans before science and technology have progressed enough to support the relevant research.

For now, this direction is retained without a concrete implementation plan. Its meaning, objectives, and possible paths will be considered as scientific discoveries and technical capabilities develop.

## Transitional goals: steps and foundations toward the ultimate goal

Within the project's goal hierarchy, wealth, emotion, artistic creation, scientific progress, population size, educational attainment, exploration and conquest of the stars, and other aspects of social development are **transitional goals** on the path to dimensional advancement.

These transitional goals provide steps and foundations for humanity's long-term survival, reproduction, and evolution. Their roles should be understood in relation to their contribution to the ultimate goal and continually reassessed as knowledge, technology, and social conditions change.

## Goal-planning approach

### Long term: random exploration and dimensional breakthroughs

The project adopts the following planning assumption: societies and technologies operating at lower dimensional levels cannot fully anticipate the state that would follow dimensional advancement in physical or informational space. A complete optimal path to the ultimate outcome therefore cannot be determined in advance.

On this assumption, the project treats random, divergent exploration as the fundamental long-term approach to dimensional breakthroughs: maintain exploration in different directions and use diverse attempts to encounter possibilities beyond existing knowledge and cognitive frameworks. Long-term planning must leave room for unknown directions and avoid premature convergence of all exploration onto one known path.

### Short and medium term: greedy selection and rapid exploration

Over short and medium horizons, the project proposes combining greedy algorithms with overall design to support rapid exploration and risk control.

Greedy selection chooses the direction assessed as best for the next step, given currently known conditions, comparable candidate directions, and established risk boundaries. After each step produces new results, subsequent actions are reassessed. These local choices depend on the knowledge and evaluation criteria available at the time and do not guarantee a globally optimal path.

### Overall design: multiple exploration subsets and risk control

At the level of overall design, the project proposes a compulsory division of humanity into multiple exploration subsets, allowing different subsets to develop in different directions and preserve a diversity of exploratory paths.

This design has two main purposes: to prevent repeated optimization against a single criterion from driving society into a local optimum, and to limit exploration and expansion so that failure along one path is less likely to spread into the destruction of the human world as a whole. “Excessive gradient descent” is used here as an analogy for the first convergence problem. Whether risks can actually be contained requires validation under the relevant conditions.

Overall design maintains multiple exploration paths and their risk boundaries; greedy selection determines the next direction at each stage; random exploration continually introduces new possible paths. The three approaches are intended to work together.

### Dynamic revision: adapt to scientific capabilities and knowledge of the ultimate outcome

The design is not fixed. As humanity's scientific and technical capabilities, knowledge of the ultimate outcome, and understanding of risks change, the project must reassess the division of exploration subsets, the allocation of directions, local evaluation criteria, expansion boundaries, and the balance between random exploration and local optimization.

“Knowledge of the ultimate outcome” means the understanding available at a given time of the final objective and the conditions for achieving it. Each stage of design rests on the knowledge then available and leaves room for subsequent revision.

## Short-term goals and approaches (10–100 years)

Relative to the long-term direction of dimensional advancement, the project treats years 10–100 after the planning effort begins as a short-term exploration window. Work in this window focuses on human physiology, social structures, and the space available for human habitation. The following research and exploration approaches are subject to revision based on evidence, technical capabilities, and actual risks.

### Goal one: evolve human physiology

Advance human physiological evolution so that emotional mechanisms and patterns of thought can more readily understand, accept, and participate in a planned society, strengthening long-term thinking, complex cooperation, and shared planning.

**Rationale:**

The project takes the view that human physiology developed through a long evolutionary history closely connected to Earth's environment and ancient survival competition. Emotional, motivational, and reward mechanisms retain characteristics associated with adaptation to scarce resources, environmental threats, and reproductive pressures.

On this basis, the project proposes that there are adaptive gaps to investigate between existing physiological mechanisms, the demands of modern society and long-term cooperation, and the ultimate goals of Light of the Future. Goal one therefore includes research into and modification of human biological mechanisms as a long-term direction, focusing on brain structure, hormone secretion, decline and aging, and genetic change across generations.

**Research and modification directions:**

- **Brain structure and thought processes:** study relationships between brain structure, neural-network organization, perception, learning, emotion, and long-term planning, exploring the development of capabilities for complex cooperation and multidimensional information processing.
- **Hormone secretion and physiological regulation:** study relationships between the endocrine system, stress responses, emotional experience, motivation, and reward mechanisms, exploring possible paths toward greater adaptability and physiological stability.
- **Decline and aging:** study mechanisms of age-related changes in physical and cognitive functions, exploring ways to extend healthy lifespan and sustain learning and long-term participation in society.
- **Genetic change across generations:** study relationships between genetic variation, transmission across generations, and environmental adaptation, assessing whether possible modifications would be heritable and examining their effects and uncertainties across generations.

**Exploration approach:**

- Study relationships between emotion, cognition, physiological conditions, and social cooperation, identifying factors affecting long-term planning, trust, conflict resolution, and collective decisions.
- Begin by exploring cognitive adaptability through education, cooperative environments, and assistive tools, then assess possible physiological directions based on research evidence. Human-subject research requires informed, voluntary participation, health protections, and independent review.
- Evaluate outcomes through health, cognitive capabilities, emotional experience, individual autonomy, and sustained cooperation, with provisions for ongoing follow-up, withdrawal, and correction.

### Goal two: optimize human social structures

Move beyond the limitations of capitalist markets in resource allocation, long-term investment, and coordination of public objectives. Explore planned societies and other forms of social organization that could support humanity's long-term evolution.

**Rationale and analytical framework:**

The project proposes the following analytical assumptions, using optimization algorithms as an analogy for social processes: under conditions where greed and short-term thinking are significant constraints on human behavior, capitalist markets can be regarded as the best-fitting solution to those conditions. They organize production and exchange through individual incentives, while remaining constrained by those incentives.

Within this framework, a market's repeated adjustments in response to local returns and feedback are likened to “gradient descent.” The project argues that this process can become locked into local optima or expand excessively along an extreme direction, creating risks of social stagnation, systemic imbalance, and potentially destructive conflict.

**Increasing complexity and its limits:**

The project characterizes one market response to these problems as “increasing complexity”: adding layers of institutions, organizations, products, and relationships to create more directions for exploration. This is likened to increasing the dimensionality of an algorithm's search space, with the intention of reducing the likelihood of society becoming locked into one path or a local optimum.

At the same time, greater complexity raises the costs of information processing, coordination, and social operation. In the project's assessment, individual pursuit of advantage and established interests may also reduce the effective scale of collective adjustment—the analogy's “smaller step size.” As systems become more complex while effective adjustment slows, further increases in complexity may eventually encounter bottlenecks this approach cannot resolve.

**Coordination between AI and goal one:**

The project seeks to use AI's information-processing, long-horizon analysis, and coordination capabilities to reduce the influence of greed and short-term thinking on resource allocation and public decisions, while lowering the coordination costs of a complex society. AI adoption must develop alongside long-term objectives, evaluation methods, and institutional design, with its effects assessed through research and pilots.

This direction complements goal one: goal one studies adaptive changes in human emotion, reward, and thought processes, while goal two studies corresponding forms of social organization, incentives, and resource allocation. The project seeks to progressively improve society's operation through the joint development of human capabilities and social structures.

**Exploration approach:**

- Develop comparable models of society and resource allocation to examine markets, public planning, and combinations of the two in long-term research, basic living provision, education, and infrastructure.
- Compare institutional complexity, coordination costs, adjustment speed, path dependence, and long-term risk within these models, testing which phenomena the algorithmic analogy can explain and where its applicability ends.
- Evaluate AI-assisted long-term planning, information integration, and resource coordination, adapting social models and incentive designs as research under goal one progresses.
- Begin with simulations and limited, voluntary pilots of public resource allocation, cooperative production, and long-term planning, retaining multiple approaches for parallel comparison.
- Continually assess resource efficiency, quality of life, innovation, distributional outcomes, participants' rights, and systemic resilience. Revise institutional designs and the scope of expansion based on observed results.

### Goal three: expand into space and establish a multiplanetary civilization with independent exploration

Progressively advance space colonization, extending human habitation, production, and knowledge accumulation across multiple planets and space habitats. When conditions permit, establish relatively autonomous centers of civilization that exchange knowledge while retaining distinct development paths, broadening the foundations for humanity's long-term survival and exploration of dimensional advancement.

**Rationale: from a single living environment to multiple development paths.**

The project takes the view that humanity's concentration on one planet, sharing highly interconnected resource and institutional systems, places common constraints on exploration and exposes humanity to certain shared risks. Expansion into space aims to increase sustainable living space and enable parallel development across different environments, technological approaches, and forms of social organization.

Goal three translates the earlier concept of “multiple exploration subsets” into a long-term direction for spatial development. Communities on different planets could develop distinct knowledge, experience, and organizational forms in their respective environments, continually producing new paths for civilization to compare, learn from, and adapt.

**Independent exploration: preserve diversity and accumulate comparable experience.**

Each center could explore resource allocation, cooperative production, education, research, and AI-assisted governance suited to local conditions. Pilots should specify objectives, applicable conditions, observation criteria, and revision procedures, recording successes, failures, and unexpected outcomes. Research involving residents requires informed, voluntary participation and provisions for withdrawal.

Centers retain knowledge exchange, shared research records, and necessary mutual assistance. When adopting results from elsewhere, each center should assess their applicability to its own environment while preserving its ability to explore different paths.

**Autonomous survival: from Earth-based supply to local sustainment.**

Expansion should follow the gradual maturation of survival capabilities, including transportation, energy, life support, food production, resource recycling, healthcare, equipment repair, and manufacturing. Early facilities may rely on support from Earth; later settlements should progressively improve local resource use, critical equipment maintenance, and resilience to supply interruptions.

Earth continues to provide essential ecological, population, and knowledge foundations. Expansion should maintain living conditions on Earth and at existing settlements so that new centers increase civilization's overall capacity to support life.

**Risk control: verify actual independence.**

The autonomy of each center must be assessed through its actual dependencies in energy, materials, production, data, governance, and AI systems. Beyond physical distance, assessments must consider correlated failures caused by shared infrastructure, common technical defects, or shared decision-making mechanisms.

The scale of expansion should match demonstrated capabilities in life support, maintenance, rescue, and risk response. For environments and technologies that remain insufficiently understood, accumulate evidence through observation, simulation, and uncrewed facilities before assessing conditions for long-term human habitation.

**Coordination with goals one and two:**

Goal one provides a research foundation for human health, cognition, and adaptation in different living environments. Goal two provides approaches to social organization and resource allocation for long-term settlement and cooperation. Goal three supplies diverse environments and independent exploration spaces for both. Observations inform revisions across all three goals in support of humanity's long-term evolution.

**Exploration approach:**

- **Build foundational capabilities:** research transportation, energy, resource recycling, and long-term habitation, testing critical capabilities through ground simulations, uncrewed facilities, and orbital infrastructure.
- **Validate limited settlements:** establish habitation and research centers of controlled scale where prerequisites are met, continually assessing health, maintenance, resource provision, and social cooperation.
- **Increase local autonomy:** progressively develop local production, repair, healthcare, and knowledge preservation, reducing reliance on a single external source for critical functions.
- **Explore different planets in parallel:** once conditions support long-term settlement on different planets, conduct relatively independent development pilots and use shared records to compare the applicability and long-term outcomes of different paths.
- **Evaluate and revise continually:** base expansion, revision, or suspension on health and quality of life, resource-recycling capabilities, endurance during supply interruptions, maintainability of critical systems, accumulated knowledge, and correlated risks.

### Phasing

| Planning window | Main work | Expected outputs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Years 10–30 | Establish foundational research, social simulations, and research into space-habitation technologies. | Reviewable research records, evaluation criteria, and candidate approaches. |
| Years 30–60 | Conduct limited pilots and technical validation where the prerequisites are met. | Comparable pilot outcomes, records of failures, and revised designs. |
| Years 60–100 | Expand mature approaches based on demonstrated capabilities and assess conditions for independent exploration on different planets. | Sustainable exploratory systems and evidence supporting further expansion, revision, or suspension. |

Progress at each stage depends on scientific evidence, conditions for participation, survival provisions, and risk-control capabilities. The three goals may be explored in parallel, with their pace and dependencies continually revised as technology and knowledge develop.
